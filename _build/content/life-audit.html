Life audit: a free worksheet and template

What is a life audit?

A life audit is a structured review of your whole life, done in one sitting. You go through each area in turn, such as work, money, health and relationships, and ask the same questions of each. How is this going? What is working? What is not? What would I change?

The value is in doing every area at once. Most of us only think hard about the part of life that is on fire. An audit makes you look at the quiet areas too, which is where the slow problems are.

How to do a life audit in 15 minutes

  1. Find fifteen quiet minutes. Phone in another room. An audit done between messages will tell you what you already think.
  2. Score each area from 1 to 10. Score how satisfied you are, not how well you are doing on paper. Go with your first number.
  3. Write what is working. One line. This is the part most people skip, and it matters. You need to know what to protect.
  4. Write what is not. One line, and be specific. "Tired" is a feeling. "In bed after midnight five nights a week" is something you can change.
  5. Choose one change for each area. Small enough to start this month. If you cannot think of one, leave it blank and move on.
  6. Pick one to do first. The worksheet lists your three lowest areas. Choose one change from those and put a date on it.

Life audit categories

This worksheet uses the eight areas most coaches use. If you have renamed the areas on the wheel of life, the audit uses your names.

  • Career. Your work, paid or not.
  • Money. Income, savings, debt and how in control you feel.
  • Health. Body, sleep, energy and mood.
  • Family and friends. The people closest to you.
  • Love. Your partner, or how you feel about not having one.
  • Personal growth. Learning and anything that stretches you.
  • Fun. Hobbies, play, rest and time off.
  • Home. Where you live and the space around you.

Life audit questions for each area

If a box on the worksheet stays empty, these questions will usually shake something loose. Three for each area.

CareerWould you apply for your own job today? Which part of your work would you keep if you could drop the rest? Is this job on the way to where you want to be in three years?
MoneyDo you know, to the nearest hundred pounds, what you spend in a month? What would three months of outgoings in the bank change? Which money decision are you putting off?
HealthHow do you feel at three in the afternoon? When did you last book a check-up without being told to? What does your body keep asking for that you ignore?
Family and friendsWho have you not seen for six months that you miss? Who do you leave feeling better, and who worse? Do the people at home get the best of you, or what is left?
LoveWhen did you last spend a whole day together with no plan? What do you need that you have not asked for? If you are single, are you content, or waiting?
Personal growthWhat can you do now that you could not do a year ago? What are you curious about and doing nothing with? When did you last feel like a beginner?
FunWhat did you do last weekend purely for enjoyment? What did you love at fifteen that you no longer do? When did you last laugh until it hurt?
HomeWhich room do you avoid? What is broken that you have stopped noticing? Does your home suit the life you have now, or the one you had five years ago?

What to do with your audit

An audit is a list of findings. It becomes useful when one of them turns into something you do.

  1. Choose one area. Usually one of your lowest two. Working on all eight at once is how audits end up in a drawer.
  2. Turn the change into a goal with a finish date. The yearly goals template breaks it into a step for every month.
  3. Tell one person. A change someone else knows about is harder to quietly drop.
  4. Print the audit and date it. In six months you will want to see what you wrote.

How often should you do a life audit?

Twice a year is about right for the full audit. The turn of the year is the obvious time. Midsummer is the other, because it is far enough from January to show whether anything has changed.

In between, a quick check keeps you honest. The wheel of life takes five minutes on the first of each month. It uses the same eight areas and lays this month's scores over last month's, so you can see which way you are moving.

Life audit or wheel of life?

They use the same areas and answer different questions. The wheel of life asks "how is each area, out of ten?" and gives you a picture. It is quick and easy to repeat. The life audit also asks why, and what you are going to do about it. It is slower and goes deeper.

Do the audit when you need to make decisions. Do the wheel when you want to keep track.

Another way: the sticky note life audit

The writer and researcher Ximena Vengoechea has a well-known version, set out in her book The Life Audit. You write a hundred wishes, one on each sticky note, sort them into themes and choose a few to focus on. It takes longer than fifteen minutes, and it suits people who think best with a pen and a wall.

The two approaches work well together. Her method is good at finding out what you want. A scored worksheet like this one is good at showing where you are now.
