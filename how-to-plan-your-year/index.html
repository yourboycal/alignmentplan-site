How to plan your year

A year plan that lasts past February has one focus, no more than three goals, and a step for every month. This guide shows you how to build one in an afternoon, with the research behind each step.

Seven stepsOne afternoonSources at the end

The short version

  1. Look back before you look forward. Plan from what last year showed you.
  2. Choose one focus for the year. A sentence, and it decides what gets your time.
  3. Set up to three goals. Specific, a stretch, and each with a finish date.
  4. Name what will get in the way. Write the obstacle next to each goal.
  5. Work each goal back into a step for every month. Start at the finish date.
  6. Turn this month's step into an if-then plan. "When X happens, I will do Y."
  7. Book the check-ins now. A minute each week, ten minutes on the first of each month.

The yearly goals template is laid out in this order and saves as you type.

Do New Year plans work at all?

Better than their reputation. One study followed 159 people who made a New Year's resolution and 123 people who wanted to make the same kind of change but had not made a resolution. Six months later, 46 percent of the resolvers said they had kept it up. Among the others it was 4 percent.1 People chose which group they were in, so this does not prove the resolution did the work. It does show that New Year plans are far from hopeless.

Making a plan is not the weak point. Most plans fail later, for reasons the next seven steps are designed to remove: too many goals, vague goals, no thought for what will get in the way, and nobody checking.

Step 1: look back before you look forward

A plan made from a blank page tends to copy last year's plan. Spend an hour on the year you have just had first. What gave you energy? What cost more than it gave? What did you keep putting off?

How to reflect on your year takes you through it in seven steps. If you are short of time, the wheel of life gives you a picture of where you are in five minutes.

Step 2: choose one focus for the year

A focus is a single sentence that says what this year is for. It is wider than a goal. It is the test you hold everything else up to.

  • "Get my health back."
  • "Build something of my own."
  • "Be around more for the people I love."

If you cannot choose between two, ask which one you would be most sorry to have ignored by next December. That one.

Step 3: set up to three goals

Now make the focus concrete. Decades of research on goal setting come to one firm conclusion: a specific, difficult goal leads to better performance than an instruction to "do your best".2 Most of that research is about work tasks, but the logic carries over. "Get fitter" is a wish. "Run 10 kilometres without stopping by 30 June" is a goal.

For each goal, write:

  • What exactly. Something you could prove to a stranger.
  • By when. A date, not "this year".
  • Why it serves the focus. If it does not, it belongs on another year's list.

Word each goal as something to start. In a Swedish study that followed more than a thousand people's New Year's resolutions for a year, 59 percent of those who set out to take something up called themselves successful, against 47 percent of those who set out to give something up.3 "Cook at home four nights a week" beats "stop ordering takeaways".

Why only three? Because every goal needs a step every month, and you have a job and a life. Three goals is thirty-six steps. That is a full year.

Step 4: name what will get in the way

Picturing success feels like progress, and on its own it changes little. What works is picturing the result and then, straight away, the obstacle between you and it. Researchers call this mental contrasting. In four experiments, people who did both committed more firmly to goals they had a real chance of reaching, and let go of goals they did not, while people who only imagined the happy ending stayed half committed either way.4 That is useful too. Better to drop a goal in January than in October.

So next to each goal, finish this sentence: "The thing most likely to stop me is..." Be honest. It is usually not time. It is usually tiredness at 7pm, or a person, or a habit you already know about.

Step 5: work each goal back into a step for every month

Start at the finish date and work backwards. If the goal is done in June, what has to be true by the end of May? April? Keep going until you reach this month. You will end up with one step for each month, and the first one will be small enough to start this week.

Then add slack. People are reliably too hopeful about how long things take. In one study, 37 students estimated how long their final-year thesis would take. On average they guessed 34 days. It took 55, and fewer than a third finished by the date they had given.5 If your plan only works when every month goes perfectly, move the finish date now.

Step 6: turn this month's step into an if-then plan

A step says what you will do. An if-then plan says when and where. The form is simple: "When X happens, I will do Y."

  • "When I finish work on Tuesday and Thursday, I will go straight to the pool."
  • "When my salary arrives, I will move £200 to savings before anything else."
  • "When I sit down with my first coffee, I will write for twenty minutes."

It looks too simple to matter. A review of 94 tests involving more than 8,000 people found that those who made a plan in this form were markedly more likely to reach their goal than people with the same goal and no such plan.6 The plan does the remembering for you.

Step 7: book the check-ins now

This is the step that separates a plan from a list. A review of 138 studies found that people who were prompted to check their progress were more likely to reach their goals, and the effect was stronger when they wrote the progress down or reported it to someone else.7 Nearly all of those studies were about health goals such as weight and exercise, but checking in is cheap and the principle is simple.

Two check-ins are enough:

  • Weekly, one minute. Did I move on this month's step? Yes, a bit, or no.
  • Monthly, ten minutes. Did the step happen? Is next month's step still the right one?

Put both in your calendar as repeating events before you close this page.

A worked example

FocusGet my health back.
GoalRun 10 kilometres without stopping by 30 June.
ObstacleI skip runs when work runs late, and it runs late most Wednesdays.
This month's stepRun and walk for twenty minutes, three times a week.
If-then planWhen I wake up on Monday, Thursday and Saturday, I put my kit on before I look at my phone.
Check-inSunday evening, one minute. First of the month, ten minutes.

What to expect along the way

It will take longer than three weeks to feel normal. The idea that a habit forms in 21 days does not hold up. When researchers tracked people building a new daily habit, the typical time for it to feel automatic was 66 days, and it ranged from 18 days to 254.8 The same study found that missing one day made no real difference. So if February is hard, that is the timetable, and you are not failing.

You will need to change the plan. A plan written in January knows nothing about your April. The monthly check-in is there so you can move a step, shrink it or swap it without giving up the goal.

When to start

January is a good time and not the only one. Searches for "diet", gym visits and sign-ups to commit to a goal all rise just after a date that feels like a new beginning: a new week, a new month, and to a smaller degree a birthday.9 If you are reading this in March, start on the first of April. Waiting for next January costs you nine months.

Put the plan where you will see it

A plan in a closed notebook is easy to forget. Pick one:

  • A 2027 year planner on the wall, with the finish dates and check-ins marked.
  • A vision board with a next step under each picture.
  • A 2027 bingo card if you would like the year to feel like a game.

Five common mistakes

  1. Too many goals. Ten goals get a tenth of your attention each.
  2. Goals with no date. "This year" means December, and December means never.
  3. Planning the result and not the first step. If you cannot do something about it this week, it is not planned yet.
  4. Keeping it to yourself. One person who will ask is worth more than any app.
  5. Treating a missed month as the end. Do next month's step. A plan has twelve chances.

Questions about planning your year

How do I plan my year?

Start by looking back at the year you have had. Then choose one focus for the coming year, set up to three specific goals with finish dates, name what is most likely to get in the way, and work each goal back into a step for every month. Book a weekly and a monthly check-in before you finish.

How many goals should I set for the year?

Three at most. Every goal needs a step every month, so three goals already means thirty-six steps across the year. One clear focus with three goals gets more done than ten goals that each get a tenth of your attention.

When should I start planning next year?

The last two weeks of December suit most people. If January has already started, pick the first of next month instead. People are more likely to act on a goal just after a date that feels like a fresh start, and the first of any month works.

What is a yearly focus?

A single sentence that says what the year is for, such as get my health back or build something of my own. It is wider than a goal and helps you decide what gets your time when everything is asking for it.

Why do most New Year's resolutions fail?

Usually because there are too many of them, they are vague, nobody has thought about what will get in the way, and nobody checks progress after January. A short list of specific goals, each with a next step and a regular check-in, fixes most of that.

How long does it take to build a new habit?

Longer than 21 days. In one study the typical time for a daily habit to feel automatic was 66 days, with a wide range from 18 to 254. Missing a single day made no real difference, so keep going after a slip.

What should I do if my plan falls apart in March?

Change it rather than abandon it. Use the monthly check-in to move, shrink or swap next month's step. A plan that changes every month is working. One that has not been opened since January is not.
