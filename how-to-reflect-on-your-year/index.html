How to reflect on your year

A year-end reflection takes about an hour and needs a pen, your calendar and your camera roll. This guide gives you seven steps in order, the questions to ask at each one, and the research behind why it works.

About an hourSeven stepsSources at the end

The short version

  1. Gather the evidence. Go through your calendar and photos, month by month.
  2. Write the year down. Three lines for each month: what happened, who with, how it felt.
  3. Mark what gave you energy and what took it. A plus or a minus next to each line.
  4. Look at the hard parts from a distance. Ask why it happened, as if it happened to a friend.
  5. Name what you are grateful for. People and moments, not categories.
  6. Choose three things to carry forward. One to keep, one to stop, one to start.
  7. Put a date on the next look. A reflection you never return to is a diary entry.

If you would like the questions laid out for you, the 45 end of year reflection questions follow the same order and save your answers as you type.

Why reflecting on your year is worth an hour

It is easy to treat reflection as a nice extra. It is closer to the opposite. Most of what you learned this year you learned the hard way: the project that slipped, the habit that stuck, the friendship that faded without anyone deciding it should. You have already paid for those lessons. Reflecting is how you collect them, and the steps below are built on what the research says about doing that well.

It also stops next year being a copy of this one. Plans made without looking back tend to repeat last year's plan, mistakes included.

Before you start

  • An hour without your phone in your hand. Two half hours on different days also works.
  • Your calendar and your camera roll. A bank statement helps too. It is an honest record of where the year went.
  • Something to write with. Paper or a screen. The writing matters more than the tool.

Step 1: gather the evidence first

Do not start from memory. Memory is a poor judge of a long stretch of time, and it gives too much weight to how things ended. In a well-known experiment, people held a hand in cold water twice: once for 60 seconds, and once for 90 seconds where the last 30 were slightly less cold. Asked which to repeat, 22 of 32 chose the longer one, even though it contained all of the same pain and more.1 When people in another set of studies rated four-year periods, they weighted the last year far more heavily than the first, although they said they meant to count each year equally.2

Your year is likely to get the same treatment. A hard November can colour a good spring.

So open your calendar at January and scroll forward. Do the same with your photos. Note anything you had forgotten. Most people find two or three months they could not have described.

Step 2: write the year down, month by month

Write twelve headings, one for each month. Under each, write three short lines:

  • What happened?
  • Who was I with?
  • How did it feel at the time?

Keep it to a list. This step is a record, and the thinking comes next.

Step 3: mark what gave you energy and what took it

Go back through your list with a pen. Put a plus next to anything that left you with more energy than you started with, and a minus next to anything that drained you. Do it quickly.

Then look at the pattern. Ask yourself:

  • What do the pluses have in common?
  • Which minus did I choose, and which was done to me?
  • What did I spend a lot of time on that has neither mark?

Step 4: look at the hard parts from a distance

Every year has something you would sooner skip. Going over it can help, or it can turn into replaying it. The difference is where you stand. In one study, people recalled a time they had been furious with someone. Those who watched the memory from the outside, as if it were happening to someone else, and asked why they felt as they did, were less angry afterwards than those who relived it through their own eyes.3 Stepping back on its own was not enough, and nor was asking why while reliving it. It was the two together.

Two ways to step back on paper:

  • Write about it using your own name or "you" in place of "I".
  • Imagine a friend had the same year. What would you say to them about it?

Then answer three questions. What happened? Why did it happen? What do I understand now that I did not then?

Step 5: name what you are grateful for

Gratitude lists have a soft reputation and solid evidence. In a ten-week study of 192 students, those who wrote a short weekly list of things they were grateful for rated their lives more highly and felt more optimistic about the week ahead than those who listed hassles or ordinary events.4 It did not make them happier day to day, but it changed how they saw the whole.

Be specific or it does nothing. "My family" is a category. "Dad driving two hours to help me move in March" is a thing that happened. Write five of those. If any involve a person, tell them.

Step 6: choose three things to carry forward

This is where the hour pays off. Read back over everything and write one line for each of these:

  • One thing to keep. Something that worked and needs protecting.
  • One thing to stop. Something that cost more than it gave.
  • One thing to start. Something the year showed you were missing.

Three is enough. A reflection that produces fifteen resolutions has produced none.

Step 7: put a date on the next look

Put a reminder in your calendar for the first of next month. Ten minutes: are the three things still true, and did anything move? The wheel of life is a quick way to do that check, because it shows this month's scores over last month's.

When you are ready to turn the three things into a plan, read how to plan your year.

Why writing it down beats thinking it through

Thinking about the year in the shower is not the same exercise. Writing forces a thought to become a sentence, and a sentence can be checked. The classic study on this asked students to write for fifteen minutes on four evenings in a row. Over the next six months, those who wrote about both the facts of a difficult experience and their feelings about it made no more trips to the health centre than before, while visits rose in every other group.5 It was a small study, and later reviews of more than a hundred similar ones found that the benefit of writing like this is real but modest.6 Modest is fine. It costs ten minutes.

You do not need to write well. You need to write in full sentences, for long enough to get past the first answer.

When to do it

The last week of December is the usual time, and there is a reason it feels right. Researchers found that searches for "diet", gym visits and sign-ups to commit to a goal all rise just after a date that feels like a new beginning: the start of a year, a month or a week, and to a smaller degree a birthday.7 They call it the fresh start effect.

That also means you have not missed it if January has started. Your birthday works. So does the first of any month. Pick the nearest one.

Reflection or rumination?

They can feel alike, and they are different things. A quick test:

ReflectionAsks "what happened and what do I do with it?" Moves forward through the year. Ends with a decision, however small.
RuminationAsks "why does this always happen to me?" Circles one event. Ends where it started, and you feel worse.

If you notice you are circling, go back to step 4 and write about it from the outside. If a memory is too heavy for that, leave it out. A year-end reflection is not the place to work through something that needs proper support.

Alone or with someone?

Do the writing alone. People edit themselves when they know someone will read it. Then, if you want to, share step 6 with one person: a partner, a friend, a colleague. Saying your three things out loud makes them real, and gives someone the right to ask you about them in March.

Five common mistakes

  1. Starting with goals. Next year's plan is better when it comes out of this year's evidence.
  2. Only reviewing work. Health, money, home and the people around you had a year too. A life audit covers all eight areas.
  3. Only listing what went wrong. You need to know what worked so you can do more of it.
  4. Doing it all in your head. See above.
  5. Never reading it again. Date it and keep it. Next December you will want to compare.

Questions about reflecting on your year

How do you reflect on the past year?

Go through your calendar and photos month by month, write three lines for each month, and mark what gave you energy and what took it. Look at the hard parts as an outsider would, name what you are grateful for, then choose one thing to keep, one to stop and one to start.

How long does a year-end reflection take?

About an hour if you follow the seven steps in this guide. Two half hours on different days work just as well. Going through your calendar and photos takes the first fifteen minutes.

What questions should I ask myself at the end of the year?

Start with three: what gave me energy this year, what cost more than it gave, and what do I understand now that I did not a year ago? If you want a full set, this site has 45 reflection questions that cover the last ten years, the last twelve months and today.

When is the best time to reflect on your year?

The last week of December suits most people because work is quiet and the new year feels like a fresh start. Your birthday or the first of any month works too. The date matters less than doing it in one or two sittings.

Is it better to write a reflection down or think it through?

Write it down. Writing turns a loose thought into a sentence you can check, and it leaves a record to compare with next year. You do not need to write well, only in full sentences.

What is the difference between reflection and rumination?

Reflection moves through the year, asks what happened and what to do with it, and ends in a decision. Rumination circles one event, asks why this always happens to me, and leaves you feeling worse. If you are circling, write about the event as if it happened to a friend.

Should I do a year-end reflection alone or with someone?

Do the writing alone, because people edit themselves when someone else will read it. Then share your three things to carry forward with one person. Saying them out loud gives someone the right to ask you about them later.
